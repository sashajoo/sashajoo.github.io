Fournier gangrene

EmergencySasha ZhuUpdated 12 Oct 2026Pocket card

Page

ED: Mr. G., 61, with diabetes. Scrotal pain and swelling for 2 days, now crepitus. HR 124, BP 94/60. Urgent urology consult.

Abbreviations: ED, emergency department; HR, heart rate; BP, blood pressure; OR, operating room; IV, intravenous; CBC, complete blood count; CMP, comprehensive metabolic panel; CT, computed tomography; CRP, C-reactive protein; ABG, arterial blood gas; ICU, intensive care unit; NPO, nothing by mouth; SGLT2, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2; ID, infectious diseases; VAC, vacuum-assisted closure; LRINEC, Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotizing Fasciitis; WBC, white blood cell count; FGSI, Fournier's Gangrene Severity Index; MRSA, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus.

#Danger signs

  • Pain out of proportion to the skin findings.2,3
  • Crepitus, bullae, dusky or black skin, or a foul smell.2
  • Fever, tachycardia, tachypnea, hypotension or organ failure are late signs of sepsis or shock.2

#First steps

  1. Go to the bedside. Examine the scrotum, penis, perineum, anus and lower abdomen.2
  2. Call your senior from the bedside. Fournier gangrene needs emergency surgery.2
  3. IV access. Give crystalloid first; your senior sets the volume.1,2
  4. Send CBC with differential, CMP, lactate and blood cultures.2
  5. Start broad-spectrum antibiotics within 1 hour. Who orders them varies by site.1,2

#Key decision

#Do not miss

  1. Fournier gangrene with sepsis or shock. In large national series, in-hospital mortality is 6 to 16% of all Fournier patients, higher with sepsis. Delay, older age and renal failure also raise it.2,5,6
  2. Early Fournier gangrene can look like simple cellulitis. Up to 75% are misdiagnosed at first.2
  3. Other emergencies with a similar picture: testicular torsion, scrotal or perianal abscess.2

#Orders and tests

  • Labs: CBC with differential, CMP, lactate, CRP, procalcitonin.2
  • ABG for oxygenation and acid-base status if he looks sick.2
  • Blood cultures now. Wound cultures later guide the antibiotic choice.2
  • Treat suspected Fournier as likely sepsis. Broad-spectrum antibiotics within 1 hour.1,2,3
  • Cover gram-positive, gram-negative and anaerobic bacteria, and MRSA.2,4
  • Add an antibiotic that blocks toxin production, per your hospital's protocol varies by site.2,4
  • Crystalloid first. Volume per your senior.1
  • Vasopressors if BP does not respond to fluids, with the ICU team varies by site.2
  • Check glucose and correct it with your senior. This matters most in diabetes.2
  • NPO now. He will likely need the OR.3
  • Stop any SGLT2 inhibitor, with your senior and the primary team.2
  • CT only if he is stable and it will not delay the OR, with your senior.2
  • Absence of gas on imaging does not rule it out.2
  • Foley if swelling prevents voiding, with your senior varies by site.2
  • Request an ICU bed with your senior. Most of these patients need ICU care.2
  • OR booking and consent go through your senior varies by site.

#Call the senior

Situation
Calling as the urology consult in the ED about Mr. G., 61, with suspected Fournier gangrene.
Background
Diabetes, 2 days of scrotal pain, worse today.
Assessment
Swollen, dusky scrotum, crepitus into the perineum. Temp 38.9 °C, HR 124, BP 94/60. Labs and blood cultures sent. ED starting fluids and broad-spectrum antibiotics.
Request
Urgent bedside review. He likely needs the OR.

#Follow up

  • Write down the times: page, first antibiotic dose, OR start.
  • Recheck lactate if it was high.1
  • Recheck vitals and urine output at the interval your unit uses varies by site.
  • In the note, mark the edges of redness and crepitus. Infection can spread beyond the visible skin edge.2
  • Document vitals, labs, the plan, and which team members were informed.
  • Handoff includes antibiotic times, pending cultures, the ICU plan and the next planned surgery.
  • When cultures return, pharmacy and ID help adjust antibiotics.2

#Normal or warning sign

Expected
ICU care after the first debridement.2
More than one surgery is expected. Severe cases often need 3.2
Expected
Open wound, often with a VAC dressing.2
Expected
The testes are spared. They have their own blood supply.2
He often becomes more stable once the necrotic tissue is removed.3
If he is not better, or is worse, after debridement, tell your senior. Another surgery may be needed.2,3
If redness or crepitus spreads beyond the wound edge, tell your senior and ask about a CT for new pockets of infection.2,3

#Avoid

  • Do not wait for CT or labs when the signs are clear or he is unstable.2
  • Do not let a lab score or a scan delay surgery. The diagnosis is mainly clinical.2,3
  • Do not call perineal redness simple cellulitis if the pain is out of proportion.2

#Study questions

What has the largest effect on survival?

Time to surgery. Early surgery can reduce mortality by half. Mortality rises with delays over 12 hours and with each 3-hour delay.2

Why give antibiotics within 1 hour if he is not in shock?

Suspected Fournier is treated as likely sepsis. Reviews ask for antibiotics "without undue delay" or "immediately".1,2,3

Why MRSA cover and a toxin blocker in Fournier?

The infection is usually polymicrobial: gram-positive, gram-negative and anaerobic bacteria. The first regimen should always cover MRSA. Staphylococcus and Streptococcus produce toxins. A protein synthesis inhibitor reduces toxin production. Cultures from the OR are then used to narrow the regimen.2,4

What is the LRINEC score for?

It uses 6 labs: CRP, WBC, hemoglobin, sodium, creatinine and glucose. It was built to separate necrotizing from other severe soft tissue infections. It does not replace the clinical exam.3

Which scores predict outcome?

FGSI uses vitals and labs. A score over 9 was linked to over 75% mortality. In comparison studies, the age-adjusted Charlson comorbidity index predicted best.2

Why are the testes usually spared?

Their blood supply comes through the spermatic cord. If the scrotum is lost, they can be placed in a thigh pouch for a while.2

Does he need a colostomy or a suprapubic tube?

Only when there is a clear reason. Fecal diversion is used if the rectum or anus is involved, and a suprapubic tube if the urethra is involved. Keep both to a minimum. They do not improve mortality.2

Is hyperbaric oxygen part of the plan?

It is only an add-on after surgery, and it must never delay debridement.2
