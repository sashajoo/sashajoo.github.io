Infected obstructing stone

EmergencySasha ZhuUpdated 12 Oct 2026Pocket card

Page

ED: Mr. R., 58, left ureteral stone on CT, temp 39.0 °C, HR 118, BP 98/60. Requesting a urology consult.

Abbreviations: ED, emergency department; CT, computed tomography; HR, heart rate; BP, blood pressure; ICU, intensive care unit; WBC, white blood cell count; IV, intravenous; CBC, complete blood count; BMP, basic metabolic panel; CRP, C-reactive protein; PT/INR, prothrombin time / international normalized ratio; PTT, partial thromboplastin time; ECG, electrocardiogram; NSAIDs, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs; GFR, glomerular filtration rate; GI, gastrointestinal; OR, operating room; IR, interventional radiology.

#Danger signs

  • Suspect an infected, obstructed kidney if a stone comes with any of these:5
    • Fever 37.8 °C (100 °F) or higher
    • WBC over 15,000
    • Pyuria
    • Hypotension
    • Unexplained tachycardia
  • Systolic BP under 90 or new confusion means likely sepsis and possible shock. Give antibiotics within 1 hour.1,3,4
  • He can look stable, then become critically ill within hours.5
    • Risk is higher with older age, diabetes, a solitary kidney or immunosuppression.5

#First steps

  1. Assess him at once and start sepsis treatment immediately.1
  2. Blood and urine cultures before antibiotics, if this delays antibiotics by less than 45 minutes.1,4
  3. Broad-spectrum antibiotics within 1 hour if shock is possible or sepsis is likely.1
    • If sepsis is possible but there is no shock, assess him and call your senior. Antibiotics within 3 hours if concern persists.1
  4. Give crystalloid first; the volume is your senior's decision.1
  5. Confirm the obstruction on imaging: stone with hydronephrosis. Call your senior now for urgent drainage.1,5

#Key decision

Ifobstructed kidney with signs of infection→then urgent drainage (stent or nephrostomy tube) plus antibiotics.5,6
Iftoo unstable for general anesthesia, or the obstruction is severe→then nephrostomy tube often preferred varies by site.5,7
Ifanticoagulant or antiplatelet drugs cannot be stopped, or severe obesity→then a ureteral stent may be needed.7

#Do not miss

  1. Septic shock from the infected, obstructed kidney, which needs drainage for source control.1,6
  2. Emphysematous pyelonephritis: gas in or around the kidney on CT, mostly in patients with diabetes.3
    • It needs drainage, glycemic control and IV antibiotics.3
  3. A solitary kidney or bilateral obstruction carries a risk of renal failure. Check potassium now.2,7

#Orders and tests

  • Blood cultures. Urinalysis and urine culture.1,4
  • CBC, BMP (creatinine, potassium), lactate.1,5
  • CRP or procalcitonin varies by site.5
  • PT/INR, PTT, platelets. Record any anticoagulant or antiplatelet drug and the last dose; these help choose between a stent and a tube.2,7
  • ECG if potassium is high.2
  • Non-contrast CT if not done. Ultrasound first in pregnancy.5
  • A normal ultrasound does not rule out obstructive pyelonephritis. CT if still in doubt.4,5
  • Foley to drain the bladder, if septic varies by site.3
  • For uncomplicated renal colic, NSAIDs such as ketorolac are often the first choice.5
    • Avoid NSAIDs, including ketorolac, in this patient because of hypotension, sepsis and rising creatinine.5,8,9
    • NSAIDs lower GFR and can worsen kidney injury.5,8,9
    • Ketorolac raises bleeding risk before a procedure.8
    • Choose the analgesic with your senior.5,8,10,11
    • Ketorolac is contraindicated with GFR under 30, past GI bleed, aspirin or NSAID allergy, or pregnancy.5
  • Admit after discussion with your senior varies by site.4
  • The procedure order, consent and the OR or IR booking are done with your senior varies by site.

#Call the senior

Situation
Urology consult in the ED for Mr. R., 58, with a left proximal ureteral stone and fever.
Background
Diabetes. Temp 39.0 °C, HR 118, BP 98/60. Lactate high, creatinine up.
Assessment
Likely infected, obstructed kidney with sepsis. Cultures sent, antibiotics in at 9:40 pm.
Request
He needs urgent drainage, so please see him now. Stent, or IR nephrostomy tube? Also, which analgesic do you want? NSAIDs are not safe for him.

#Follow up

  • Recheck vitals and urine output frequently. Repeat a high lactate, often at about 6 hours varies by site.1,5
  • At drainage, send renal pelvis urine for culture. It predicts urosepsis better than bladder urine.2
  • Aim to move a patient in shock to the ICU within 6 hours. Arrange it with your senior.1,2
  • IV antibiotics until cultures guide the choice, then narrow varies by site.3
  • Stone treatment later, once the infection clears. Often within 1 to 2 weeks varies by site.5,7
  • At handoff, include the drain type and side, the stone still in place, the culture source and the plan for stone surgery.
  • Stents and tubes need a planned exchange or removal.2
  • Document the times of sepsis recognition, cultures, antibiotics and drainage.

#Normal or warning sign

Expected
Clear improvement within 24 to 48 hours of treatment.3
Minor, short-lived bleeding in the nephrostomy tube is expected (seen in about 95%).2
If there is no improvement by 48 to 72 hours, call your senior and repeat imaging.3
  • Look for an abscess, a wrong antibiotic, or a kidney that is still obstructed.3
Bright red, pulsatile blood from the tube: call your senior now.2
  • It needs urgent angiography and embolization.2
If BP falls after drainage, call your senior.
  • The procedure itself can cause sepsis.2

#Avoid

  • Do not treat the stone while the kidney is infected. Drain the kidney now and treat the stone later.5,7
  • Do not rely on antibiotics alone.5,6
  • Do not wait for him to be "more stable" before drainage.4

#Study questions

Which drain do you ask for, a stent or a nephrostomy tube?

Both drain the kidney.

A stent needs general anesthesia, and it may not pass the stone.

A tube needs local anesthesia and sedation. It is preferred when the patient is unstable.5

Why not remove the stone during the same procedure?

Instrumenting an infected, obstructed kidney can push bacteria into the blood and worsen sepsis.2,7

So drain now with minimal manipulation, and treat the stone after the infection clears.5

The urinalysis is nearly clean. Can the kidney still be infected?

Yes.

With the kidney obstructed, the urine may show no clear sign of infection.

Base the decision on the whole clinical picture.3
